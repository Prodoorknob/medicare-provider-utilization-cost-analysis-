Section 01

Project Overview & Goal

This project builds a two-stage machine learning pipeline that answers a question no public tool currently answers well: "Given my Medicare coverage, my doctor's specialty, and the procedure I'm having — what will I actually pay out of pocket?"

Stage 1 uses the CMS Provider Utilization and Payment Data (2013–2023, 11 years, all 50 states) to predict the Medicare allowed amount for any provider × HCPCS × geography combination. A time-series LSTM layer adds a forecasting dimension — predicting what allowed amounts will look like in future years by specialty.

Stage 2 takes the Stage 1 prediction as an input and uses the Medicare Current Beneficiary Survey (MCBS) to model the distribution of patient out-of-pocket spending conditional on supplemental coverage type, demographics, and chronic condition burden. The output is a probabilistic range, not a single number — communicating real uncertainty to real patients.

The core portfolio thesis

This project demonstrates the full data science stack: distributed data engineering (Databricks + S3 + PySpark), experiment tracking (MLflow), three distinct modeling families (GLM, tree ensembles, deep learning), and deployment thinking (what does the output actually need to look like for a patient to act on it?). The MCBS integration makes this meaningfully different from any of the dozens of "predict Medicare costs" Kaggle notebooks.

What "patient-facing" means in practice

The end-state deployment is a simple query interface where a user provides: their supplemental coverage type, their doctor's specialty, the procedure they're having, and their state. The system returns an estimated Medicare allowed amount (Stage 1), a projected cost range for 2025–2026 (LSTM), and a patient out-of-pocket range with a confidence interval (Stage 2).

Data Schema — Gold Layer Feature Matrices

The gold layer produces two separate feature matrices — one per data source — that are joined at inference time, not at training time. Training each stage independently prevents data leakage and allows each stage to be retrained independently as new data arrives.

Why log-transform the target in Stage 1?

The distribution of allowed amounts is heavily right-skewed — a small number of high-cost procedures (infusions, complex surgeries) dominate the variance and make RMSE a misleading metric on the raw scale. Log-transforming the target gives the model a more symmetric error distribution and means RMSE is interpretable as approximate percentage error. Back-transform predictions with exp() at inference time.

Model Specifications

Stage 1 — Allowed Amount Models

ModelRationaleKey HyperparametersEvaluation
GLM (Ridge) Interpretable baseline. Coefficients directly quantify the dollar effect of specialty and geography. Required for inference analysis. alpha (L2 penalty), feature scaling required RMSE, MAE, R², paired t-test vs. tree models
Random Forest Captures non-linear interactions between specialty and HCPCS without manual feature engineering. Good OOB error estimate. n_estimators, max_depth, min_samples_leaf Same metrics + feature importance (MDI)
XGBoost Expected best predictive performance. SHAP values provide interpretability to complement raw accuracy. learning_rate, n_estimators, max_depth, subsample, colsample_bytree Same metrics + SHAP global/local
LSTM (PyTorch) Captures temporal autocorrelation in year-over-year allowed amounts. Only model that produces forward forecasts. hidden_size, num_layers, dropout, seq_length, lr Forecast RMSE on held-out years, PI coverage

Stage 2 — Out-of-Pocket Models

ModelRationaleOutput Type
GLM (Linear) Interpretable baseline showing direct effect of coverage type on OOP Point estimate (mean)
Gradient Boosting Captures non-linear interactions (e.g., coverage type × allowed amount × chronic conditions) Point estimate (mean)

MLflow tracking strategy

Both stages share a single MLflow experiment on Databricks for unified visibility. Each model run logs: all hyperparameters, train/val/test metrics, the serialized model artifact, a feature importance plot, and (for Stage 2) the quantile coverage plot. This makes the full pipeline reproducible from a single MLflow UI view.

Section 06

Risks & Mitigations

High — MCBS Geographic Resolution

MCBS public files only provide census-region geography (4 regions), vs. state-level in CMS data. Stage 2 loses geographic granularity. Mitigation: Clearly communicate this in the app UI; offer state-level Stage 1 output separately from the regional Stage 2 output.

High — Medicare Advantage Blindspot

54% of Medicare patients are in MA plans not captured by either dataset. Mitigation: Scope the app explicitly to "Traditional Medicare (Original Medicare) beneficiaries" and add a clear MA disclaimer to the UI.

Medium — MCBS Sample Sparsity

Rare coverage type × specialty × region combinations may have very few MCBS observations, producing wide and unreliable quantile estimates. Mitigation: Flag cells with n < 30 observations with a "low confidence" indicator in the app output.

Medium — Annual Retraining Requirement

Medicare fee schedules update every January. CMS releases prior-year data ~18 months after year end, so 2024 data arrives mid-2025. Mitigation: Build the pipeline to accept new annual files with a single config change. LSTM forecasts partially mitigate staleness.

Low — Databricks Free Tier Compute

Free tier serverless compute cannot use s3a:// or mount DBFS. Already solved via pandas + HTTPS URL pattern. Mitigation: Pattern is documented in existing notebooks; MCBS ingestion will follow same approach.

Low — LSTM Sequence Length

11 years of data is a short sequence for an LSTM. The model may overfit or produce unstable forecasts. Mitigation: Use 1D CNN + LSTM hybrid or add auxiliary features (specialty, volume) as context. Evaluate forecast intervals carefully on held-out years.

How to frame limitations in the portfolio writeup

The limitations aren't weaknesses — they're evidence of domain understanding. A candidate who says "our model predicts OOP for Traditional Medicare FFS patients, which represents 46% of beneficiaries; extending to MA would require CMS encounter data that isn't publicly available" demonstrates significantly more sophistication than one who ignores the gap entirely. Lead with what the model does, follow with what it doesn't, and explain why the gap exists structurally.
