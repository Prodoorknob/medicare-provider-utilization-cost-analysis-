01 // WHY XGBOOST + CATBOOST + LIGHTGBM?

The V1 Problem

V1 trained Random Forest, XGBoost, and GLM on 30% of the data (38M rows) using regional batching due to 24 GB WSL2 RAM constraints. The best V1 result was RF at R2=0.884. GLM diverged entirely (R2=-102). The question for V2: can we do better by (a) using all the data and (b) choosing models with complementary inductive biases?

Model Selection Rationale

XGBoost: The Proven Baseline

SELECTED Level-wise tree growth, L1/L2 regularization, histogram binning. V1 had already shown XGBoost works on this data (R2=0.833 at 30% sample). The V2 hypothesis was that full data would close the gap with RF, and it did: R2=0.945. XGBoost's hist method on GPU processes 126M rows in 8 minutes, making it the fastest of the three.

CatBoost: Native Categorical Handling

SELECTED WITH CAVEATS The key motivation was ordered target statistics for the 7 categorical features, especially HCPCS_Cd_idx (~6,000 unique procedure codes). Standard label encoding (used by XGBoost) treats code 4500 as "between" 4499 and 4501, which is meaningless. CatBoost computes optimal splits by building statistics from randomly permuted subsets, avoiding target leakage that naive target encoding introduces.

The theory was sound but the practice was harder. CatBoost GPU has restrictions: no rsm (column sampling) for regression, subsample requires bootstrap_type='MVS' instead of the default Bayesian, and convergence on 126M rows with high-cardinality categoricals is 3-4x slower than XGBoost. At 1,000 iterations it scored only R2=0.789. At 3,000 iterations: R2=0.907. Still the weakest of the three, suggesting the ordered target statistics add value but the model needs more iterations or depth to fully exploit them at this scale.

LightGBM: Leaf-Wise Growth + GOSS

BEST PERFORMER Two architectural differences from XGBoost made LightGBM the winner:

1. Leaf-wise growth (num_leaves=63) instead of level-wise (max_depth=6). For 126M rows with high-cardinality categoricals, leaf-wise finds deeper, more specific splits in the high-density regions of the feature space without wasting capacity on sparse regions. XGBoost's level-wise approach builds balanced trees that allocate equal depth to every branch, even those with few samples.

2. Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) retains all samples with large gradients (hard examples) and randomly samples from small-gradient instances. On 126M rows, most instances are "easy" (common procedures, standard pricing). GOSS focuses training capacity on the ~20% of hard cases, which is where the incremental R2 comes from.

Additionally, LightGBM natively handles categorical features (optimal histogram splits) without the overhead of CatBoost's ordered permutations. On CPU with all cores, LightGBM trained 1,000 rounds in ~50 minutes vs CatBoost's ~100 minutes per 1,000 iterations on GPU.

The Avg_Sbmtd_Chrg Question

Finding: V1 RF attributed 61.8% feature importance to Avg_Sbmtd_Chrg. We hypothesized that removing it would drop R2 by 0.25-0.30, revealing how much the model was simply learning "allowed = f(billed)". The actual drop was only 0.015.

Why the drop was small: The hcpcs_target_enc feature (mean allowed amount per procedure code, fitted in a separate pass on training data) encodes nearly the same pricing information as the submitted charge, but through procedure identity rather than the specific bill. Together with HCPCS_Cd_idx, these two features capture the Medicare fee schedule structure. The charge is redundant once you know what procedure was performed.

Implication: The no-charge model (R2=0.943) is deployable for prospective cost estimation where the billed charge isn't known yet. This is the more useful model for real-world applications.

An RMSE/MAE ratio near 1.0 means uniform errors. A ratio near 2.0+ means the model has a long tail of large errors on specific cases. All V2 models show ratio ~2.0, indicating that the remaining error is concentrated in outlier cases (rare procedures, unusual geographic pricing, edge-case provider types) rather than uniform noise. The models are good at common cases and bad at rare ones, which is the expected pattern for a fee-schedule-driven target variable.

Why CatBoost Underperformed

Root cause: Insufficient iterations for convergence at scale.

CatBoost's ordered target statistics compute per-feature optimal splits using random permutations of the training set. With 126M rows and 6,000 unique HCPCS codes, each iteration explores a massive combinatorial space. The convergence curve shows:

CatBoost was still improving at iteration 2,999 (best_iteration = 2999). XGBoost converged by round ~500. LightGBM plateaued around round ~800. CatBoost's slower convergence is the price of its more principled categorical handling, which builds richer internal representations but needs more passes to refine them.

Additionally, GPU restrictions forced removing rsm (column subsampling), which reduces regularization and may cause slight overfitting on the training permutations. CatBoost on CPU with rsm=0.8 might converge faster at the same R2, but CPU training on 126M rows would take prohibitively long.

Three Reasons for Negligible Ensemble Lift

1. Correlated Error Patterns

All three models are gradient boosted decision trees operating on the same 13 features. They make the same mistakes on the same hard cases: rare HCPCS codes with few training examples, geographic outliers (territories with tiny populations), and provider types with high cost variance (anesthesiology, interventional cardiology). Stacking can only average away uncorrelated errors.

2. LightGBM Already Captures the Signal

With R2=0.9575, LightGBM explains 95.75% of variance. The remaining 4.25% is likely irreducible noise (within-provider billing variance, patient mix differences, year-over-year fee schedule changes not captured in the features). XGBoost and CatBoost cannot recover signal that doesn't exist in the feature set, so they add only noise to the ensemble.

3. CatBoost Weakness Dragged Down Diversity

For stacking to work, base learners need to be both accurate and diverse. CatBoost at R2=0.907 is accurate enough, but its errors are a superset of LightGBM's errors (it fails on everything LightGBM fails on, plus more). The negative Ridge weight (-0.023) means CatBoost's predictions are being subtracted, not added. The meta-learner uses CatBoost as a small correction term, not a contributor.

Verdict: The ensemble (R2=0.9580) adds +0.0004 over LightGBM alone (R2=0.9576) at a cost of 13.3 hours and ~149 CU. LightGBM is the production model. The ensemble provides no practical benefit and should not be deployed.

05 // THE REAL V2 WIN: DATA VOLUME

Same Model, Different Data Scale

XGBoost0.83310.9452+0.112

Same hyperparameters, same features, same split strategy. The only difference is data volume: 38M rows vs 126.8M rows. This +0.112 R2 gain is entirely from seeing more data, not from model architecture changes. The V2 improvement story is fundamentally about removing the WSL2 memory constraint, not about CatBoost or LightGBM being better algorithms.

Lesson: For datasets with high-cardinality categoricals (6K procedure codes x 100 specialties x 63 states), the model needs to see enough examples of each combination to learn stable splits. At 30% sampling, many combinations had too few examples for reliable estimation. At 100%, coverage is sufficient. This is a data coverage problem, not a model capacity problem.

07 // RECOMMENDATIONS

For Production Deployment

Deploy LightGBM V2 (R2=0.9575, MAE=$6.73) as the Stage 1 model. It is the best single model and the ensemble adds negligible value. The no-charge variant (R2=0.9428) is the better choice for prospective estimation where the billed charge isn't available.

For Future Iteration

If pursuing ensemble gains, the path is model diversity, not more boosters. Consider:

1. Neural network base learner (TabNet, FT-Transformer) for structurally different error patterns
2. Per-specialty sub-models that specialize on high-variance procedure groups
3. Residual stacking where the second model predicts LightGBM's residuals, not the target directly

For Stage 2 OOP (Next Sprint)

The remaining ~89 CU is sufficient for V2_04 through V2_08. The OOP synthetic data is 10.3M rows (12x smaller), and the TFT forecasting data is 23K groups. Neither will repeat the compute overrun. Budget 3x the initial estimate as a safety margin.
